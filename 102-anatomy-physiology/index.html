    The cell, its skin and its jelly (plasma membrane and protoplasm)

    SyllabusCell structure – Plasma membrane and protoplasm

    The cell is the smallest unit of life. A thin skin holds a living jelly inside.

    Think of it like thisA cell is like a water balloon with a guard at the door. The balloon skin is the plasma membrane. The water inside is the protoplasm. The guard lets some things in and keeps others out.
    Remember
    1. Everything inside the cell is protoplasm.
    2. Protoplasm = cytoplasm (the jelly outside the nucleus) + nucleoplasm (the jelly inside the nucleus).
    3. The outer skin is the plasma membrane: two layers of fat with proteins in it.
    4. It is selectively permeable. It lets some things in and keeps others out.
    For the exam — what to write

    The cell is the smallest unit of life. Everything inside the cell is called protoplasm. Protoplasm = cytoplasm (the jelly outside the nucleus) + nucleoplasm (inside the nucleus).

    The plasma membrane is the outer wall. It is a double layer of fat (phospholipid bilayer) with proteins floating in it. This is the fluid mosaic model. It is selectively permeable: it lets some things in and keeps others out.

    Answer plan — "Describe the structure of a cell"

    1. Define cell and protoplasm (2 lines).
    2. Draw the cell. Label at least 8 parts (see Point 3).
    3. Plasma membrane: fluid mosaic model, selectively permeable.
    4. Write each organelle with its job (the table in Point 3).
    5. End: all organelles work together to keep the cell alive. This is homeostasis at the level of the cell.

    How things cross the cell skin (osmosis and diffusion)

    SyllabusProcess of Osmosis & Diffusion

    Diffusion moves any particle from crowded to less crowded. Osmosis moves only water, across a membrane.

    Think of it like thisSpray perfume in one corner. Soon the whole room smells of it. That is diffusion. Put a raisin in water. It swells, because water goes in. That is osmosis.

    Diffusion

    Any particle moves from high to low concentration. No energy. No membrane needed. Example: O₂ moves from the alveoli into the blood. CO₂ moves out.

    Osmosis

    Water only moves through a semi-permeable membrane. It goes towards more salt or sugar. No energy. Example: the large intestine absorbs water.
    Remember
    1. Both need no energy.
    2. Diffusion: any particle. No membrane needed.
    3. Osmosis: water only, through a semi-permeable membrane.
    4. In osmosis, water goes from a weak solution to a strong solution.
    For the exam — what to write

    Make this table — "Differentiate osmosis and diffusion"

    If time — active transport

    Active transport moves particles from low to high concentration. It needs energy (ATP). Example: the sodium–potassium pump.

    Unit 1 · Point 4 of 7Must learn

    Keeping the inside steady (homeostasis)

    SyllabusHomeostasis

    Homeostasis means the body keeps its inside steady, even when the outside changes.

    Think of it like thisA room with an air conditioner. When the room gets too hot, a sensor feels it. The machine cools the room, then stops. Your body does the same with heat, sugar and blood pressure.
    Change — body temperature rises after Surya Namaskar
    ↓
    1 · Receptor (sensor) — skin and brain sense the heat
    ↓
    2 · Control centre — the hypothalamus in the brain decides
    ↓
    3 · Effector — sweat glands sweat, skin vessels widen
    ↓
    Response — temperature falls back to 37 °C
    ↺ the response cancels the change = negative feedback
    Remember
    1. Every control loop has 3 parts: receptor → control centre → effector.
    2. Negative feedback (most common): the response reverses the change.
    3. Positive feedback (rare): the response makes the change bigger, until an event ends.
    4. Steady values: temperature about 37 °C · blood pH 7.35–7.45 · fasting blood sugar about 70–100 mg/dL.
    For the exam — what to write

    Walter B. Cannon gave the word "homeostasis". Claude Bernard first wrote about the steady "internal environment".

    Negative feedback (most common)

    The response reverses the change. Examples: body temperature · blood pressure (baroreceptor reflex) · blood sugar (insulin).

    Positive feedback (rare)

    The response increases the change until an event ends. Examples: childbirth (oxytocin) · blood clotting.

    Yoga link

    Ayurveda defines health as balance (Sama): balanced doshas, digestive fire (Agni), tissues (Dhatu) and waste (Mala), with a pleasant self, senses and mind (Sushruta Samhita, Sutra Sthana 15). This is the old name for homeostasis.

    Yoga practice moves the nervous system towards the "rest and digest" (parasympathetic) side. This helps the body return to balance.

    Research

    • Pascoe 2017 (meta-analysis, 42 RCTs): yoga with asanas lowered cortisol, systolic BP, resting heart rate, fasting sugar, cholesterol and LDL.
    • Tolahunase 2017 (12 weeks, n = 96): yoga and meditation lowered free radicals (ROS), a DNA-damage marker, cortisol and IL-6. Telomerase rose. Cell-level ageing markers improved. No control group.

    Answer plan — "What is homeostasis? Explain feedback."

    1. Definition + Cannon + Bernard.
    2. The 3 parts: receptor, control centre, effector. Draw the loop.
    3. Negative feedback with the temperature or BP example.
    4. Positive feedback with childbirth or clotting.
    5. Yoga link: Ayurveda's "Sama" idea of health. Yoga supports the parasympathetic side.

    Not in old papers (new unit). The syllabus outcome CO2 asks you to "describe the concept of homeostasis", so expect a question.

    Nerve cells with and without a fat coat (myelinated and non-myelinated neurons)

    SyllabusNervous tissues (Myelinated neuron and Non-myelinated neuron)

    Some nerve cells wear a fatty coat. The coat makes the signal jump, so it travels fast.

    Think of it like thisA frog crosses a pond. Jumping from stone to stone is fast — that is a myelinated nerve. Swimming the whole way is slow — that is a non-myelinated nerve.

    Myelinated

    Axon wrapped in a fatty myelin sheath. Gaps in the sheath = nodes of Ranvier. The signal jumps node to node (saltatory conduction). Fast.

    Non-myelinated

    No myelin sheath. The signal moves along every part of the axon (continuous conduction). Slow.
    Remember
    1. Parts of a neuron: dendrites (receive) → cell body → axon (carries the signal away) → axon terminals.
    2. Nervous tissue = neurons (carry signals) + neuroglia (support cells).
    3. Myelinated = fast, jumps. Non-myelinated = slow, continuous.

    Unit 1 · Point 7 of 7Must learn

    The body's electricity (bio-electric potential)

    SyllabusBio-electric Potential

    A resting nerve cell is negative inside. A nerve signal is a quick flip to positive, then back.

    Think of it like thisA row of dominoes. Push the first one hard enough, and the whole row falls. Push too softly, and nothing happens. A nerve signal is like that: all or nothing.
    Remember
    1. At rest, the inside is about −70 mV.
    2. The sodium–potassium pump keeps it: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in for each ATP.
    3. Threshold about −55 mV. Peak about +30 mV.
    4. All-or-none law: if the push reaches threshold, the full signal fires. If not, nothing fires.
    For the exam — what to write

    Resting membrane potential: the inside of a resting neuron is negative, about −70 mV. The sodium–potassium pump keeps it: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in for each ATP.

    Action potential = the nerve signal. It follows the all-or-none law. Phases: depolarisation (Na⁺ rushes in) → repolarisation (K⁺ moves out) → hyperpolarisation → rest again. It lasts about 2 ms.

    If time — machines that record these potentials

    ECG (heart) · EEG (brain) · EMG (muscle). Yoga researchers use them to measure the effect of practice.

    Research

    Telles 2024 (n = 22): during high-frequency yoga breathing, a brain potential to sound (the Pa wave of the auditory evoked potential) grew larger.

    Answer plan — "Resting membrane potential and action potential"

    −70 mV at rest · the Na⁺–K⁺ pump (3 out, 2 in) · threshold −55 mV · the 3 phases · all-or-none law · draw the graph.

      SyllabusDigestive system - buccal cavity, pharynx, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, anus

      Food travels down one long tube. Each part of the tube does one job.

      How we breathe in and out (mechanism of breathing)

      SyllabusMechanism of breathing (expiration and inspiration)

      The chest gets bigger, so air rushes in. The chest gets smaller, so air goes out.

      Think of it like thisA syringe. Pull the plunger back: the space grows, and liquid rushes in. Push it: liquid goes out. Your diaphragm is the plunger.

      Breathing in (inspiration) — active

      The diaphragm contracts and moves down. The external intercostal muscles lift the ribs up and out. Chest volume ↑, so lung pressure ↓. Air flows in.

      Breathing out (quiet expiration) — passive

      The muscles relax. The diaphragm moves up. The lungs spring back like a balloon. Chest volume ↓, so lung pressure ↑. Air flows out.
      Remember
      1. Boyle's law: when chest volume goes up, pressure inside goes down. Air flows from high to low pressure.
      2. Breathing in is active. Quiet breathing out is passive.
      3. Forced breathing out is active: internal intercostal and belly muscles push the air out. This happens in Kapalbhati.
      4. Normal rate at rest: about 12–16 breaths a minute.
      For the exam — what to write

      External respiration

      Gas exchange between the alveoli and the blood in the lungs. O₂ goes into the blood. CO₂ comes out.

      Internal respiration

      Gas exchange between the blood and the body cells. O₂ goes into the cells. CO₂ comes out. Cells then use O₂ in mitochondria = cellular respiration.

      Kapalbhati: a forceful breath out (active) and a passive breath in.

      Respiratory control centre

      Centres in the medulla and pons of the brain stem control breathing. Chemoreceptors sense CO₂, H⁺ and O₂ in the blood. A rise in CO₂ is the strongest signal to breathe more. You can also control breathing by will — this is why pranayama is possible.

      Air and blood must meet (ventilation–perfusion ratio, V/Q)

      SyllabusVentilation-perfusion ratio

      V/Q compares the air reaching the air sacs with the blood flowing past them. Normal is 0.8.

      Think of it like thisA shop needs both customers and goods. Customers with no goods: no sale. Goods with no customers: no sale. Air and blood must both arrive, or no gas is exchanged.

      V/Q high → "dead space"

      Air arrives, but blood does not. Example: a clot blocks a lung artery.

      V/Q low → "shunt"

      Blood arrives, but air does not. Example: a blocked airway or pneumonia.
      Remember
      1. V = air that reaches the alveoli each minute, about 4 L.
      2. Q = blood that flows through the lungs each minute, about 5 L.
      3. V/Q = 4 ÷ 5 = 0.8 (normal).
      4. When you stand, the top of the lung (apex) has a high V/Q. The bottom (base) has a low V/Q.

          Inside a moving joint (structure of a synovial joint)

          SyllabusStructure of synovial joints

          In a synovial joint, two smooth bone ends sit in a bag of oily fluid.

          Think of it like thisThe hub of a bicycle wheel, packed with grease. The grease cuts friction, so the wheel turns smoothly. Synovial fluid is the grease of the joint.
          Remember
          1. 6 parts: bone, articular cartilage, cavity with fluid, joint capsule, synovial membrane, ligament.
          2. The synovial membrane makes the fluid.
          3. The fluid oils the joint, feeds the cartilage and absorbs shock.
          4. Cartilage has no blood supply. Movement pushes the fluid in and out to feed it.
          For the exam — what to write

          Synovial fluid oils the joint, feeds the cartilage and absorbs shock. Cartilage has no blood supply. It gets its food from this fluid, and movement helps the fluid move in and out.

          Some joints also have menisci (cartilage pads in the knee) and bursae (small fluid bags that cut friction).

          Yoga link

          Joint rotations in Sukshma Vyayama come first in a session. Gentle movement warms the joint and moves the synovial fluid before deeper asanas.

          Research

          Lauche 2019 (meta-analysis, 9 RCTs): in knee osteoarthritis, yoga reduced pain and stiffness and improved function. The evidence quality was very low.

          How a muscle gets shorter (mechanism of muscle contraction)

          SyllabusMechanism of muscle contraction

          Inside a muscle, thin and thick threads slide over each other. So the muscle gets shorter.

          Think of it like thisPush two combs into each other. The teeth slide in, and the combs come closer. The teeth do not get shorter. This is the sliding filament theory.
          Remember
          1. Muscle → fascicle → muscle fibre (cell) → myofibril → sarcomere. The sarcomere (Z line to Z line) is the part that shortens.
          2. Thin thread = actin. Thick thread = myosin.
          3. Nerve signal → acetylcholine → Ca²⁺ is released → myosin grabs actin and pulls.
          4. ATP lets myosin let go and pull again.
          For the exam — what to write

          Sliding filament theory: Huxley and Hanson · Huxley and Niedergerke, 1954.

          The 8 steps

          1 · Nerve signal reaches the neuromuscular junction
          ↓
          2 · Acetylcholine (ACh) is released
          ↓
          3 · Muscle action potential spreads over the cell and into the T-tubules
          ↓
          4 · The sarcoplasmic reticulum releases Ca²⁺
          ↓
          5 · Ca²⁺ binds troponin — tropomyosin moves aside, binding sites on actin open
          ↓
          6 · Myosin heads grab actin (cross-bridge) and pull = power stroke — ATP lets the head let go and pull again
          ↓
          7 · The sarcomere shortens — Z lines come closer; I band and H zone get shorter; A band stays the same
          ↓
          8 · Relaxation — acetylcholinesterase breaks ACh; Ca²⁺ is pumped back (needs ATP); tropomyosin covers actin again

          Neuromuscular junction

          The meeting point of a motor nerve ending and a muscle fibre. The nerve releases acetylcholine into the small gap. Acetylcholine binds receptors on the muscle (motor end plate) and starts the muscle action potential. Example: the nerve to the biceps makes the biceps contract when you bend the elbow.

          Yoga link — effect of asanas on muscle

          • Holding an asana = isometric work: the length stays the same and tension builds → strength and endurance.
          • Moving into and out of an asana = isotonic work: the muscle length changes.
          • A slow stretch in asanas → flexibility.
          • Asanas with relaxation (Shavasana) lower muscle tension.
          • Tran 2001 (n = 10, 8 weeks of hatha yoga): strength rose by 19–31%, knee-flexion endurance by 57% and trunk flexibility by 188%. A small study.

          If time — rigor mortis: after death there is no ATP, so myosin cannot let go, and the muscles stay stiff.
